It's the first question almost every client asks at a site visit, usually before we've even got the ladder to the loft hatch: *"Will we need planning permission?"*

The short answer, for most homes, is **no**. The majority of loft conversions in England fall under **permitted development rights**, which means you can build without submitting a planning application at all. But there are exceptions, and getting it wrong is expensive, so here's how the rules actually work.

## Permitted development: the basics

Permitted development (PD) rights let you extend and alter your home within set limits without applying for planning permission. For loft conversions, the key limits are:

- **Volume:** the conversion can add up to **40 cubic metres** of roof space on a terraced house, or **50 cubic metres** on a semi-detached or detached house. A typical rear dormer sits comfortably within this.
- **Height:** nothing can extend beyond the highest point of the existing roof.
- **The front slope:** dormers and extensions on the roof slope facing the road are *not* permitted development. That's why you see so many rear dormers and so few front ones.
- **Materials:** the new work has to be similar in appearance to the existing house.
- **Balconies:** raised platforms and full balconies aren't PD. Juliet balconies (glazed doors with a guard rail, no platform) usually are.

If your conversion stays within all of those limits — and most dormer, hip-to-gable and Velux conversions do — you don't need planning permission.

## The exceptions

There are a few situations where the PD route is off the table:

1. **Conservation areas and Article 4 directions.** If your street is in a conservation area, roof extensions usually need a full planning application. Some councils also remove PD rights on specific streets with an Article 4 direction.
2. **Flats and maisonettes.** PD rights apply to houses only. If you own a flat, every loft conversion needs planning permission.
3. **Mansard conversions.** Rebuilding the whole rear roof slope almost always exceeds PD limits, so mansards nearly always go through planning.
4. **Homes where PD has already been used up.** The volume allowance covers the house as it was originally built. If a previous owner added a big rear extension under PD, some of your allowance may already be spent.

## What you need instead: building regulations, always

Here's the part people mix up: **every loft conversion needs building regulations approval**, whether or not it needs planning permission. Building regs cover the structural work, fire escape routes, stair design, insulation and sound-proofing — and you'll need the completion certificate when you come to sell the house.

It's also worth getting a **lawful development certificate** even when you build under PD. It's a formal confirmation from the council that the work was lawful, and it saves awkward questions from buyers' solicitors years down the line.
