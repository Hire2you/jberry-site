When clients first call about an extension, most have a single-storey kitchen extension in mind. And often that's the right call. But at the site visit I'll usually ask one question that changes the conversation: *"Have you thought about going up as well as out?"*

Here's why — and how to work out which is right for your home.

## The economics: two floors share one roof

The most expensive parts of any extension are the groundworks and the roof. Foundations, drainage, steels, roof structure, tiling and weatherproofing account for a large slice of the budget — and here's the thing: **a double-storey extension uses the same foundations and the same roof as a single-storey one.**

That second floor is mostly walls, floor joists, windows and finishes — the cheap part. So while a double-storey extension costs more in total, the **cost per square metre is significantly lower**. As a rough guide from our own projects:

- A single-storey rear extension typically comes in between **£55,000 and £85,000**.
- A double-storey version of the same footprint typically comes in between **£90,000 and £120,000** — not double the price, for double the space.

If you need an extra bedroom or bathroom as well as a bigger kitchen, building both at once is nearly always better value than doing them as two separate projects years apart.

## When single-storey is the right answer

Value per square metre isn't everything. A single-storey extension is usually the better choice when:

- **The ground floor is the whole problem.** If the bedrooms are fine and it's the cramped kitchen that's driving you mad, don't buy space you don't need.
- **You want the glass-box feel.** Rooflights, lantern roofs and full-width glazing are what make modern kitchen extensions special — and they need a single-storey roof to sit in.
- **Planning is tight.** Many single-storey rear extensions fall under permitted development. Double-storey extensions almost always need a full planning application, and they're more likely to draw objections from neighbours about overlooking and overshadowing.
- **Budget is fixed.** A smaller project done to a high standard beats a bigger one built to a squeezed spec, every time.

## When double-storey earns its keep

Go up as well as out when:

- **You need a bedroom or bathroom anyway.** This is the big one. If a loft conversion or a move is on the cards within five years, one double-storey build now is cheaper and less disruptive than two projects later.
- **The house is unbalanced.** Plenty of three-bed semis have a decent ground floor and one bathroom for five people. A double-storey extension fixes the actual problem.
- **You're staying long-term.** The extra disruption and planning time pay back over the years you'll live in the finished house.

## What about value?

Agents will give you precise-sounding figures, but the honest version is this: **extra bedrooms and bathrooms move a house up a price bracket; a better kitchen sells the house faster within its bracket.** If your goal is resale value, double-storey usually wins. If your goal is loving the house you're in, the kitchen you use fifty times a day might matter more than a bedroom you don't currently need.
